# Five Lamps — Diwali wall stories (draft 2, fact-checked)

Copied from the Claude Doc "Five Lamps — Diwali wall stories" (https://claude.ai/artifact/KxaAE51VTbBgZ4bHZRnmcA) on 4 Nov 2026. The doc is the binding source; this copy keeps the words safe in the repo.

## Opening
We come into this life with a forgetting. Diwali is the night we help each other remember.

Five old stories, five lamps, from the darkest end of this wall to the brightest. In each one, someone forgot who they were. Watch who reminds them.

## 1 · Narakasura — The Son Who Forgot
He was born of the Earth herself. Bhudevi, the goddess of the ground beneath every foot, was his mother, and he was given a gift: no one could end him but her.

Somewhere along the way he forgot whose son he was. He built a kingdom out of taking. He took the earrings of Aditi, mother of the gods. He took sixteen thousand women and locked them away. The more he took, the less he remembered, and the darker his city grew.

When Krishna came against him, Satyabhama rode beside him in the chariot, and Satyabhama was Bhudevi, born again. So it was his mother, in the end, who undid him. The gift that kept him safe was the same thread that brought him home.

As he fell, Narakasura knew her. His last wish was not for his kingdom. He asked that his ending be kept with lamps, so the day would be remembered for its light, not mourned.

So one of Diwali's lamps is lit for the one who had been darkest. Across much of the South, Naraka Chaturdashi is Deepavali itself.

Forgetting is not where the story ends. Sometimes it is where the way back begins.

*When did you last lose sight of yourself, and who reminded you?*

## 2 · Lakshmi — What the Ocean Was Keeping
Once, the gods grew careless with a blessing. The sage Durvasa cursed them for it, and Lakshmi (fortune, beauty, everything that makes a life full) slipped away from them and sank into the ocean of milk.

To bring her back they had to work with the very ones they feared. Gods on one end, asuras on the other. A mountain for a churning rod, the great serpent Vasuki for a rope, and the whole ocean between them.

The first thing to rise was poison, dark enough to end the world. Shiva drank it and held it in his throat. That is why his throat is blue.

They kept churning.

Treasures began to surface: a cow that grants wishes, a tree that grants wishes, the moon. And at last, seated on a lotus, Lakshmi rose. She looked past both ends of the rope and chose Vishnu.

What they had lost was never gone. It was waiting below, for whoever kept going past the poison.

On Diwali night we clean our homes, open our doors and light the way in, so she can find us again.

*What came up for you after a hard stretch that you didn't expect?*

## 3 · Mahavira — The Light That Was Already Inside
Mahavira spent his life learning to see with nothing in the way: no possessions, no harm to any living thing, no clinging. Through his teaching, many found their own way to that clear seeing.

On the new-moon night of Kartik, at Pavapuri, he left his body and was freed for good. Jains call it his nirvana.

Eighteen kings had gathered there. They understood that a great light had gone out of the world, so they lit lamps. If the light of knowing had left, they would hold small lights in its place.

His closest disciple, Gautama, was not there; Mahavira had sent him away to teach. When the news reached him, he grieved. And in the grieving he saw it: he had been holding on to his teacher. He let go. Before that night was over, his own complete knowing opened.

The lamps outside were lit for a teacher who had gone. The light that stayed was the one inside the student all along.

*What's something you understand now that you couldn't see before?*

## 4 · Bandi Chhor — Fifty-Two Threads
Guru Hargobind, the sixth Sikh Guru, was held in the fort at Gwalior. Inside its walls he found fifty-two kings, prisoners too, some held so long they had stopped expecting to leave.

When the emperor Jahangir agreed to release the Guru, the Guru said he would not leave alone. The emperor set a condition he thought would end the matter: whoever could hold on to the Guru's cloak as he walked out could go with him.

So the Guru had a cloak made with fifty-two tassels. One for every king. They walked out together, each one holding a thread.

Sikh tradition remembers that he reached Amritsar at Diwali, and the Harmandir Sahib was lit with lamps to welcome him. Sikhs keep the day as Bandi Chhor Divas: the day of release.

A light kept for yourself is a lamp. A light you carry out for others becomes a city.

*Who did you bring out of the dark with you?*

## 5 · Rama — Coming Home
For fourteen years Rama lived in the forest, a prince without a palace. Sita was taken from him. He crossed the sea to Lanka, fought, and brought her back.

And there, at the end of the war, the gods came and told him who he was. You are Vishnu, they said. You were never only a prince.

In Valmiki's telling, Rama answered: I think of myself as a man, Rama, son of Dasharatha. Tell me who I am.

Even he had forgotten.

Then he walked home. Tradition remembers it as the darkest night of the month, the new moon of Kartik, with nothing in the sky to guide anyone. So the people of Ayodhya filled the city with clay lamps, on every roof and wall and road, until the whole city was one light and he could find his way.

The gods told him who he was. His people lit the way back.

That is the story every lamp tonight is telling. We are not becoming something new. We are finding our way back to what we already were, and the people who love us light the road.

*What did you forget that you already were, and what brought you back?*

## Closing
Every lamp in this room was lit from another. Thank you for bringing yours.
